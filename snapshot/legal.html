PART XV - THE INTERNATIONAL VERDICT

15.1 Recognition of Palestine

157 of 193 UN member states now recognise the State of Palestine - over 81% of the international community. The recognition now extends to 14 of the 19 member states of the G20 (Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Canada, China, France, India, Indonesia, Mexico, Russia, Saudi Arabia, South Africa, Türkiye and the UK), plus permanent invitee Spain - meaning that recognition of Palestine is no longer the position of the developing world alone but the settled majority view of the world's largest economies.

Recent waves:

  • September 2025: UK, Canada, Australia, Portugal, France, Belgium, Luxembourg, Malta, Monaco, Andorra, San Marino - the largest single-month recognition wave in history, including multiple permanent UN Security Council members
  • May 2024: Ireland, Norway, Spain
  • 2024: Slovenia, Armenia, Mexico, Bahamas, Trinidad and Tobago, Jamaica, Barbados

France's recognition in September 2025 is particularly significant: France is a permanent member of the UN Security Council and a major Western power. Combined with the UK's recognition, two of the five permanent Security Council members now formally recognise Palestinian statehood.

The remaining 36 states that do not recognise Palestine are predominantly the United States, Germany, Italy, Japan, South Korea, and a smattering of Pacific and Caribbean micro-states historically aligned with US foreign policy. They constitute a small minority of the international community and do not represent the global consensus.

15.1A The Third Advisory Opinion: Obligations Towards the United Nations and Relief Agencies (22 October 2025)

The Advisory Opinion of 19 July 2024 on the illegality of the occupation is recorded at §15.1. It is not the Court's last word. On 22 October 2025 the International Court of Justice delivered a third Advisory Opinion, Obligations of Israel in relation to the Presence and Activities of the United Nations, Other International Organizations and Third States in and in relation to the Occupied Palestinian Territory (ICJ General List No. 196; UN document A/80/502), requested by General Assembly resolution 79/232 of 19 December 2024, adopted with 137 member states in favour. The request followed the Knesset legislation of October 2024 directed at dismantling UNRWA's operations.

What the Court held. The Opinion was unanimous on most of its findings, with the remainder carried ten votes to one, Vice-President Julia Sebutinde dissenting principally on the points concerning UNRWA. President Iwasawa Yuji summarised the central holding: "As an occupying Power, the State of Israel has an obligation under international human rights law to respect, protect and fulfil the human rights of the population of the Occupied Palestinian Territory, including through the presence and activities of the United Nations, other international organizations and third States." The Court found that Articles 55 and 56 of the Fourth Geneva Convention require the occupying power to ensure the population receives the essentials of life, and that where supplies are inadequate Article 59 obliges it to agree to relief schemes and to facilitate them. It held that the duty goes beyond permitting passage: an occupying power must use all means at its disposal so that relief items are distributed in a regular, fair and non-discriminatory manner (para. 101). On the agency specifically, it held that Israel cannot ban UNRWA consistently with its obligations as occupant, because "it is not possible to replicate the capacity of UNRWA" and the agency "cannot be replaced on short notice and without proper transition plan". It further held that Israel must cooperate with the United Nations under Article 2(5) of the Charter and cannot invoke sovereign prerogatives in territory over which it has no sovereignty, and it recalled the prohibition on starvation as a method of warfare (paras. 143-145).

15.1B "Gaza Genocide: A Collective Crime" - The Special Rapporteur on Third-State Complicity (20 October 2025)

Two days before that Opinion, on 20 October 2025, the UN Special Rapporteur on the situation of human rights in the Palestinian territories occupied since 1967, Francesca Albanese, submitted to the General Assembly "Gaza Genocide: a collective crime" (A/80/492). Where her 2024 report Anatomy of a Genocide addressed the conduct, and her 2025 report From Economy of Occupation to Economy of Genocide (A/HRC/59/23) addressed the corporate layer recorded at §13.8, this report addresses the states.

Its thesis is that the genocide is not a crime Israel committed alone. It finds that influential third states, with the acquiescence of many others, sustained it through diplomatic, military, "humanitarian" and economic support, and in some cases through active participation, within a framing the report describes as colonial narratives that dehumanise Palestinians. Two of the states it names are already documented in Part XIII of this report from independent sources: Germany, identified as the second-largest arms exporter to Israel during the period, supplying, in the report's phrase, from "frigates to torpedoes"; and the United Kingdom, identified by reference to more than 600 surveillance flights over Gaza since October 2023, a figure independently established by the AOAV and Declassified UK flight-tracking analysis recorded at §13.3A.

[Recorded with the same qualification this report applies to every special-procedures report: a Special Rapporteur's report is a mandate holder's assessment submitted to the General Assembly, not a judicial determination, and its evidentiary weight lies in its sourcing rather than in its status. Its relevance here is that it is the first UN report to frame third-state conduct as participation in the crime rather than as a failure of policy, and that the two states it particularises are the two whose supply and surveillance records this report documents independently at §13.3 and §13.3A.] (Francesca Albanese, "Gaza Genocide: a collective crime", A/80/492, 20 October 2025; OHCHR, "Gaza genocide: A crime Israel did not commit alone, says Special Rapporteur", October 2025; Al Jazeera, 28 October 2025.)

UN experts (April 2026) called for the immediate suspension of the EU-Israel Association Agreement as the "minimum requirement under international law," citing continued arms transfers and Israel's failure to comply with the ICJ Advisory Opinion and provisional measures.

The UK's continued partial, not full, embargo (as of 8-9 September 2026). The UK's own arms-control position remains a partial embargo, first imposed September 2024: roughly 30 of 350 extant export licences suspended over risk of International Humanitarian Law breaches, with the remainder - including a disputed exemption for F-35 stealth fighter components - left intact. That exemption survived a legal challenge: in June 2025, the High Court dismissed a case brought by Al-Haq and the Global Legal Action Network, accepting the government's argument that F-35 parts manufactured in the UK are supplied into a multilateral collaborative pool (with the US, Italy and Japan among the assembly-line partners) rather than sold directly to Israel, and that then-Defence Secretary John Healey had judged suspending the licences would "undermine US confidence in the UK and NATO." Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband's 8 September 2026 statement added a "double lock" - a commitment to refuse all future licence applications that materially contribute to the occupation - without extending the suspension to the F-35 exemption itself. Nearly three years into the war on Gaza, and notwithstanding the 8 September settlement sanctions package (§15.3), the UK has therefore still not moved to a full two-way arms embargo - the demand made since 2024 by the UK TUC (above), the Green Party, Humza Yousaf, and the Palestinian Youth Movement Britain, among others. (High Court of Justice; Al-Haq/GLAN; gov.uk; CNBC; The National, 8-9 September 2026.)

Continued RAF military flights to Israel under Burnham (July-September 2026). Flight-tracking data compiled by independent journalist Matt Kennard and reported by Novara Media on 3 September 2026 documented at least ten military flights departing RAF bases for Israel in the first six weeks of Andy Burnham's premiership, which began on 20 July 2026. Eight Stratotanker refuelling aircraft departed RAF Mildenhall in Suffolk for Israel between 24 July - four days after Burnham entered Downing Street - and 26 August 2026. An Airbus A400M Atlas, a transport aircraft capable of carrying up to 37 tonnes of cargo or 166 personnel and equipment including armoured vehicles and helicopters, departed RAF Akrotiri in Cyprus and landed in Tel Aviv on 26 August 2026; the Ministry of Defence told The National this flight was to resupply embassy staff but declined to comment on the flights originating from RAF bases in the UK itself. On 11 August 2026, a Boeing C-17 transport aircraft operated by the US military departed RAF Lakenheath, also in Suffolk, for Israel. Burnham has additionally granted US President Donald Trump permission to use British military bases in support of strikes against Iran. Before becoming prime minister, Burnham said he was "sorry" for the way Labour had handled Israel's war on Gaza but declined to characterise it as a genocide, and Novara Media's analysis concluded he "has so far not departed from the party's policy of allowing arms exports to and military support for" Israel. The flights sit within a documented pattern: the UK flew 60 aircraft to Israel in the months following 7 October 2023, per Kennard's earlier reporting for Declassified UK (May 2024), alongside the more than 600 RAF surveillance missions over Gaza already documented at §13.5. The continuation of resupply and refuelling flights through the six weeks immediately following the 8 September 2026 settlement sanctions announcement (§15.3) illustrates the same gap between declaratory policy and underlying military cooperation that characterises the F-35 exemption above: a change of prime minister, and a formal ethnic-cleansing finding announced by his own Foreign Secretary, coinciding with no interruption to the flow of RAF logistical support to Israel. (Novara Media, 3 September 2026; Matt Kennard/Declassified UK; The National.)

Critical commentary on the sanctions-versus-military-support gap. The disjunction between the UK's declaratory settlement sanctions and its continued military logistical support has itself become a subject of public critique. Journalist Chris Corney, commenting on Israel's retaliatory closure of the British Consulate-General in Jerusalem (§15.3), wrote that "the purpose of this massive faux outrage by Israel is to bolster Burnham with left voters by making it look like he is actually doing something," arguing that "in practice, Burnham's Labour govt will go on supporting Israel's violent expansion, even providing military cover" and cautioning readers "don't fall for it." The claim that Israel's diplomatic retaliation is calibrated, in part, to enhance the domestic political standing of the government it is retaliating against cannot be independently verified from Israeli sources, and is offered here as documented public commentary rather than an established fact. It is, however, consistent with the documentary record above: the same six-week period in which Israel closed its consulate, expelled UK ceasefire monitors and barred UK parliamentarians (§15.3) is the same period across which RAF refuelling and transport flights to Israel continued without interruption, and across which the F-35 components exemption remained untouched by the sanctions package. 15.3 Sanctions on Israeli Officials

June 2025: UK, Australia, Canada, New Zealand, and Norway formally sanctioned Israeli ministers Itamar Ben-Gvir and Bezalel Smotrich for incitement of settler violence and statements promoting forcible displacement.

11 May 2026: The European Union imposed its first comprehensive sanctions package on Israeli settlers: travel bans and asset freezes on seven settlers and settler organisations. The measure had been blocked for years by Hungary under Viktor Orbán. Following Orbán's electoral defeat in April 2026 and his replacement by Peter Magyar's government, Hungary lifted its veto. The 27-member EU bloc's first settler sanctions package signals a significant shift in European diplomatic pressure.

June 2026 - Western sanctions over the E1 settlement: Britain, together with other Western states, moved to sanction any company involved in the E1 settlement project - the development specifically designed to split the West Bank in two and foreclose Palestinian statehood - and to designate further entities supporting settler violence (Middle East Eye, June 2026). Nine states, including the UK, France and Australia, had jointly warned in late May 2026 that no company should participate in E1. 8 September 2026 - the twelve-country settlement goods ban and Miliband's "ethnic cleansing" statement. Fulfilling the trajectory set by the May and June 2026 measures above, twelve states - France, the United Kingdom, Canada, Denmark, Spain, Finland, Ireland, Iceland, Norway, Poland, Portugal and Sweden - jointly announced coordinated sanctions on Israeli settlements in the West Bank. 15.3A British Charitable Funds and the Settlements: The Regulatory Record

This subsection records a strand of the United Kingdom's conduct that is domestic rather than diplomatic and is therefore easily separated from the measures at §15.1 to §15.3: the flow of British charitable money, and in part of British tax relief, to organisations operating in settlements that the same government states are illegal.

The government's stated position, and the obligation it carries. Successive British governments have held that Israeli settlements in the occupied Palestinian territories are unlawful. In September 2026 Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband stated that the government's position is that Israel's occupation of the Palestinian territories is now unlawful in its entirety, bringing the United Kingdom into line with the International Court of Justice's Advisory Opinion of 19 July 2024, which held the occupation unlawful and held that states are obliged "not to render aid or assistance in maintaining the situation created by Israel's illegal presence" in the territory. Under the Charities Act 2011 a registered charity's funds must be applied to charitable purposes for the public benefit.

The parliamentary complaint. In June 2026, Labour MP Melanie Ward alleged in a formal complaint to the regulator that 32 charities registered in England and Wales had sent at least £28 million to Israeli settlements, and that the British taxpayer may have subsidised up to £5.6 million of that through Gift Aid, the relief under which the state adds twenty-five per cent to a qualifying donation.

The statutory inquiry. On 5 August 2026 the Charity Commission for England and Wales opened a statutory class inquiry under section 46 of the Charities Act 2011 into charities working in illegal Israeli settlements, beginning with eight unnamed charities selected by the location of their partners and the recency of the alleged activity. Its stated scope is to establish the extent to which funds were applied in relation to settlements, the purposes for which they were used, and whether that met the requirements of charity law. Stephen Roake, assistant director of high-risk compliance, said: "Serious allegations have been made about UK charities operating in illegal Israeli settlements in Palestine, so our first step is to establish the facts. Only then can we determine if regulatory action is required." The Commission stated that it intends to extend the number of charities under investigation over time, has shared the scope with the police and HM Revenue and Customs, and will consider naming the charities when it reports. It stressed that opening an inquiry is not a finding of wrongdoing.

The Declassified investigation of 15 September 2026. Declassified UK, reporting by Melissa Müller, identified six further registered British charities openly fundraising for activities and infrastructure in settlements, which between them appear to have sent more than £1.7 million to recipients in settlements over three years, a figure the outlet states could be much higher. Two of the six state in their own Charity Commission filings that they support named settlements; others carry a settlement name in their own title. Four are linked to Modiin Illit, the largest settlement in the West Bank by population, commonly called Kiryat Sefer: the Gainsborough Trust, whose stated objects include benefiting "inhabitants of Modiin Illit," and which registered income above £448,000 between 2021 and 2025; British Friends of Ezrat Yisrael Kiryat Sefer, whose income exceeded £21 million across the same period, including £4.2 million in 2025, and which gave £3.3 million to the Israeli food-distribution body Oneg Shabbos, which states on its own website that it supports hundreds of settler families in the occupied West Bank; Friends of Kupat Hakahal Kirhat Sefer, which raised over £1.65 million and whose principal recipient, matched on the Israeli government-approved Guidestar register, lists financial assistance to Israeli soldiers among its registered activities; and Matan Beseiser Kiryat-Sefer Beis Aron, which states in its filings that it operates "in practice in Kiryat Sefer, Israel." Two further charities support other settlements: The Friends of the Yeshivat Har Etzion Trust, which handled donations above £500,000 between 2021 and 2025 and is listed by the school in Alon Shvut as one of four international bodies collecting for it, the school describing itself as one of the largest hesder institutions, where students combine army service with study; and British Friends of Ariel University, raising for the university in the settlement of Ariel, which calls itself a "beacon of Zionism," states that some 4,500 of its students were serving in the war on Gaza, and thanked the charity and Israel's then ambassador Tzipi Hotovely for an event held at the ambassador's residence in July 2025. None of the charities responded to the outlet's inquiries; contact details filed with the regulator for five of the six did not work, and a letter to the Gainsborough Trust was returned undelivered because it was no longer at its registered address. Shaima Dallali, legal researcher at the International Centre of Justice for Palestinians, said: "The UK cannot simply acknowledge that settlements are illegal while turning a blind eye to financial flows originating within its own jurisdiction that may contribute to sustaining them," and that "the government and regulators have been far too slow to confront the systematic issue of UK charitable funds supporting settlements." The ICJP has filed a complaint with the Commission and the Metropolitan Police about one of the six and says it is reviewing the others; the Met told the outlet it had received 248 referrals relating to the war since October 2023 and that no UK-based investigation relating to the conflict was then under way. (Declassified UK, 15 September 2026; Charity Commission for England and Wales, 5 August 2026; GOV.UK press notice; Civil Society News, 5 August 2026; The National, 5 August 2026; ICJP, 6 August 2026.)

[The evidentiary status of this subsection is stated expressly. Nothing here is a finding that any named charity has broken the law: the regulator has said in terms that its inquiry is fact-finding and is not a finding of wrongdoing, the charities did not respond to the reporting, and several of the identifications rest on name and address matching between the British filings and the Israeli register rather than on admissions. 15.3B The Measures Tested: The Legal Profession's Assessment, the Enforcement Gap, and the State's Own Contracts

This subsection records what happened to the 8 September 2026 sanctions package at §15.3 in the week after it was announced, when three bodies of evidence were brought to bear on it: an assessment by the United Kingdom's own legal profession of whether the measures discharge the obligations the government had just accepted; a documented account of whether an import ban of this design can be enforced at all; and the same state's existing contractual relationships with companies the United Nations identifies as operating in the settlements. The three are recorded together because each tests the same proposition from a different direction, and because the government's own statement of position, that the occupation is unlawful in its entirety, is what makes all three checkable rather than rhetorical.

The letter of 15 September 2026. On 15 September 2026, one week after the Commons statement, more than 130 lawyers, former judges and legal academics sent a six-page letter to Prime Minister Andy Burnham stating that the announced sanctions fail to meet the United Kingdom's obligations under international law. Signatories include the barrister Michael Mansfield KC; Sir Alan Moses, former Lord Justice of Appeal; Nimer Sultany, professor of law at SOAS; and Ralph Wilde, professor of international law at University College London. The letter welcomes the decision to act alongside Canada and France and then states that "the action covered in the recent announcement falls far short" of Britain's obligations to oppose the occupation under the International Court of Justice's Advisory Opinion of 19 July 2024. Its central legal proposition concerns the consequence of the government's own finding: having stated at the despatch box that the occupation is unlawful and that ethnic cleansing is being perpetrated in the West Bank, "the UK Government cannot treat that knowledge as absent." The letter sets out five classes of measure it regards as required: extending the sanctions to insurance, accountancy services and digital infrastructure, the service layers on which settlement commerce depends and which the announced package leaves untouched; public confirmation that no UK public body, pension scheme or sovereign fund holds investments in settlements; a full arms embargo on Israel, invoking Article 6(3) of the Arms Trade Treaty, which prohibits a transfer where the state has knowledge that the items would be used in genocide, crimes against humanity or grave breaches of the Geneva Conventions; steps to ensure that funds raised by UK-registered charities do not support settlement activity (the subject matter of §15.3A); and the application to Israel of the same breadth of measures the United Kingdom applied to Russia - banks, energy, shipping, exports, investment and financial services. Professor Wilde called the acknowledgement of illegality "welcome" while stating that the duties it triggers are "far more wide-ranging" than the steps announced. Professor Sultany put the structural objection more directly: "The elephant in the room is that the occupation is not a bug, it's a feature." (Middle East Eye, 15 September 2026.)

The precedent within the profession. The September 2026 letter is the fourth in a documented sequence from the same constituency, and the earlier letters establish that the position is not new and is not marginal within the English legal profession. On 26 October 2023 and 3 April 2024 open letters concerning Gaza were sent by UK-based or UK-qualified lawyers. On 26 May 2025 a further open letter, published with a supporting Legal Memorandum, carried 1,137 numbered signatories, grouped by the organisers as 12 members of the former judiciary, 86 King's Counsel, 142 founders, partners and directors of firms and legal organisations, 69 professors, 372 other barristers, 326 other solicitors and 130 other legal academics. Its signatories included the former Supreme Court Justices Lord Sumption and Lord Wilson; the former Lords Justices of Appeal Sir Anthony Hooper, Sir Alan Moses and Sir Stephen Sedley; Michael Mansfield KC; Baroness Chakrabarti; Imran Khan KC; Gareth Peirce; Martyn Day; Professor Christine Chinkin; and Professor Guy S. Goodwin-Gill. Describing themselves as "lawyers, legal academics and former judges who are UK-based or qualified," the signatories stated that "genocide is being perpetrated in Gaza or, at a minimum, there is a serious risk of genocide occurring" - the formulation that tracks the Bosnia v Serbia trigger at Part VI rather than awaiting a merits judgment - and concluded that "The UK's actions to date have failed to meet those standards." The five measures sought in 2025 were a ceasefire and the resumption of aid, the lifting of the UNRWA ban, sanctions on Israeli ministers and personnel, a trade review including suspension of the 2030 Roadmap, and a commitment to execute the ICC arrest warrants; arms sales were deliberately left aside because the High Court proceedings at §15.2 were then pending. (UK Judges' and Lawyers' Open Letter Concerning the Occupied Palestinian Territory, 26 May 2025, with signatory list and Legal Memorandum; earlier letters of 26 October 2023 and 3 April 2024.)

The enforcement gap, as documented before the ban was announced. Whether an origin-based import ban can be enforced is not a matter of opinion, because the question had already been investigated. On 10 June 2026 the Global Echo Litigation Center published Importing Occupation: Europe's Complicity in Palestinian Dispossession through Settlement Agricultural Trade, the product of a four-year investigation analysing more than 30,000 Israeli export documents covering eight years of agricultural shipments to Europe, cross-referenced against government data, field investigation and interviews with senior figures at Israeli exporters, packing houses and producers. Of 5,900 shipments dispatched between October 2017 and February 2026, more than 17 per cent contained products originating in settlements - roughly one in six overall and close to one in five of those bound for the European Union - against the figure of 2.23 per cent previously cited on Israel's own account, which the report concludes is not reliable. Of 2,040 invoice declarations and movement certificates examined, 340 appear to provide ineligible proofs of origin and a further 20 use a "sham address" inside Israel's sovereign territory to declare Israeli origin falsely. The report identifies three mechanisms by which settlement goods reach European shelves labelled "Product of Israel" and claiming the preferential tariff status reserved for Israeli goods: "Hiding in Plain Sight," "Sham Address" and "Mingling," the last being the consolidation of settlement produce with Israeli produce at Israeli packing houses. Its stated conclusion is that settlement goods in the European supply chain are "neither marginal nor exceptional." Global Echo has commenced legal action in the United Kingdom, including a matter concerning HM Revenue and Customs and settlement-origin imports that may have improperly received preferential treatment under the UK-Israel Trade and Partnership Agreement. (Global Echo Litigation Center, Importing Occupation, 10 June 2026, executive summary and press release; Agence Europe.)

What the ban reaches, and what it does not. Measured against that record, the announced ban is narrow in four documented respects. First, scale: UK-Israel trade in goods and services was approximately £6 billion in 2025 and the free trade agreement remains in force, while UK-Palestine trade for the four quarters to March 2026 was £40 million, of which imports into Britain were about £6 million - a ceiling equal to roughly 0.1 per cent of annual UK-Israel trade. Second, measurement: the government concedes it is "difficult to obtain accurate figures for UK trade" with the settlements, because the statistics separate Israel from Palestine but not settler-produced goods from Palestinian-produced goods, and the principal import categories - fruit and vegetables, vegetable oils, coffee, tea and cocoa - are grown by both, so Britain is prohibiting imports whose value it cannot quantify. Third, enforcement: no detailed policing plan has been published, and the existing instruments are HMRC's list of ineligible locations, proof-of-origin documents naming postcode and locality, and, since September 2025, customs code Y864, by which an importer declares that goods did not originate in territory occupied after June 1967. Jess Stober, legal director of Global Echo, identified the change in incentive rather than in detection: "Under the old system, if an importer was caught, the tariff could be paid and the goods could still be sold," whereas under a prohibition goods "may be refused entry" and firms face loss and sanction, which gives supply-chain actors "a reason to conduct proper origin due diligence." Fourth, scope: services - finance and insurance, logistics, legal work and tourism - are covered only by a pledge to "take action" and not by the prohibition itself, and UK exports to the settlements, which constitute most of the trade flow, are excluded altogether. The measures are expected to take six to nine months to come into force. (Al Jazeera, 8 and 9 September 2026; HMRC guidance; Global Echo.)

The state's own contracts. The fourth test is the one the government applies to itself. An Al Jazeera investigation of 28 August 2026, using procurement data compiled by the analysts Tussell, found that 17 companies and entities linked to Israeli settlements hold 125 United Kingdom public-sector contracts with a combined award value of £2.129 billion (about $2.89 billion). Five companies ultimately controlled by Motorola Solutions Inc - Airwave Solutions Ltd, Motorola Solutions UK Ltd, CRFS Ltd, 3TC Software Ltd and Noggin IT Ltd - hold 91 active contracts worth £1.726 billion, of which the largest single item is a Home Office extension worth £1.562 billion for the secure communications network used by police, fire and ambulance services across England, Scotland and Wales; Motorola Solutions UK separately holds £123.9 million, including a £36.5 million Ministry of Defence contract for radios, accessories and airtime. The remaining contracts sit within four corporate groups: Heidelberg Materials (Germany); the French engineering group Egis, general consultant to the Jerusalem light rail network connecting settlements in occupied East Jerusalem to West Jerusalem, which has formally disputed its inclusion in the UN database; the Spanish train manufacturer CAF, which holds an £83.5 million contract with the West Midlands Combined Authority for urban trams and owns 50 per cent of TransJerusalem J-Net Ltd, awarded a €1.8 billion project to construct and extend light-rail lines through East Jerusalem with construction continuing into 2027; and the Chinese conglomerate Fosun, linked through a subsidiary to the cosmetics company Ahava, which has operated in the Mitzpe Shalem settlement, and whose subsidiary Breas Medical holds two UK public contracts worth £1.29 million. All five corporate groups are identified in the United Nations Human Rights Office database of business enterprises involved in activities related to the settlements. Stephen Humphreys, professor of international law at the London School of Economics, stated that Britain may be failing to meet its legal obligations by not opening its own investigation into these activities. The investigation was published while more than 140 Labour MPs were calling for a ban on trade with the settlements. Asked in September whether the sanctions would require the review, restructuring or termination of these contracts, the government did not answer. Jess Stober framed the point as one of internal consistency: London has now expressly accepted that the occupation is unlawful, and if policy is to align with the ICJ's July 2024 Advisory Opinion, the government "should also examine whether its own procurement and contracting relationships are consistent." (Al Jazeera, 28 August and 9 September 2026; Tussell; UN Human Rights Office database of business enterprises, updated 2023.)

[The evidentiary status of this subsection is narrow and is stated to prevent overreading. It is not a finding that the United Kingdom is in breach of the Advisory Opinion, which is a determination for a court and not for this document, and it is not a finding that any named company has broken any law; inclusion in the UN database is a statement that an enterprise is involved in listed activities, not an adjudication, and one listed company disputes its inclusion. Nor does this subsection treat the 8 September package as worthless: it is the most substantial British measure against settlement commerce on record, and §15.3 records it as such. What the material establishes is the gap between a stated legal position and the conduct of the state that states it, measured on the state's own figures. The government has accepted that the occupation is unlawful in its entirety, which under the Advisory Opinion carries the obligation not to render aid or assistance in maintaining it. In the same period it prohibits an import flow it cannot quantify and estimates at about £6 million, exempts the services and the export flow that make up most of the relationship, allows six to nine months before the prohibition binds, and holds £2.129 billion in live contracts with companies the United Nations lists as operating in the settlements. The 130 signatories are not making a political argument about the desirability of sanctions; they are making a legal argument that a state which has published a finding cannot then act as though it had not, and the four bodies of evidence above are the material against which that argument can be tested by a reader without reference to anyone's characterisation of it.]

15.3C The Response to the Measures: An Israeli Ministerial Letter Recommending Entry Bans on British Aid Organisations (8-21 September 2026)

The subsections above record what the United Kingdom did on 8 September 2026 and the legal profession's assessment that it fell short. This subsection records what the Israeli state did in response, on the same day, which is material because it bears directly on the question §15.3B leaves open: whether the announced measures were substantial enough to matter.

The letter. On 8 September 2026 - the day the United Kingdom announced its ban on imports from the illegal settlements - Israel's Ministry for Diaspora Affairs and Combating Antisemitism issued an official letter recommending that border authorities bar from entry to Israel the employees and activists of a list of British organisations. The document, first reported by the BBC and the Jerusalem Post and published in detail by Al Jazeera on 17 September 2026, names eleven organisations. They include the humanitarian agencies Oxfam, Islamic Relief Worldwide, Save the Children, Medical Aid for Palestinians (MAP) and the Norwegian Refugee Council - the last of which receives United Kingdom government funding - alongside the campaigning organisations Palestine Solidarity Campaign, Friends of Al-Aqsa and the proscribed Palestine Action. The letter's stated rationale is that "attention should be directed to the ongoing and subversive activities of various British organisations promoting boycotts, sanctions, and economic and political measures against the State of Israel." The particulars advanced against individual organisations include advocating engagement with Hamas for the purpose of humanitarian access, demanding an arms embargo on Israel, alleged ties to the Muslim Brotherhood, and alleged work with organisations the letter characterises as terrorist. Save the Children is listed on the ground that it described Israel's conduct as "deliberate starvation" and "genocide" - characterisations which, as Parts VI and VII of this report record, are those of the UN Commission of Inquiry, the IPC-based famine determination, Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch and a majority of the International Association of Genocide Scholars. Friends of Al-Aqsa is listed by reference to its founder Ismail Patel's attendance at demonstrations and "harsh statements against Israel." Palestine Action is described as "a network of radical branches promoting direct action intended to damage the Israeli defence industry."

The responses. Ismail Patel said that "Israeli restrictions will not intimidate us." Palestine Action co-founder Huda Ammori - whose challenge to the proscription is before the Supreme Court on expedited appeal following the grant of permission on 30 July 2026 (§13.9A) - said the letter demonstrates that "we were not banned because of anything to do with 'terrorism.'" On 21 September 2026 the Muslim Council of Britain, the largest umbrella body of British Muslim organisations, issued a statement on the letter which opened not with the letter but with a death: "This past week, an Israeli drone killed 8-year-old Wafaa Akila and her father in Gaza City - hours after she'd gone back to school for the first time this year. She is amongst thousands of Palestinian children killed since the war began." (The strike is recorded at Part VII above and occurred on 7 September 2026; the MCB's "this past week" is a loose temporal reference rather than a date claim.) The statement continued: "Now Israel is targeting and calling for a ban on aid and humanitarian advocacy organisations that rush to save lives in the aftermath of attacks and or highlights Israeli violations," and characterised the letter as "an obvious retaliation for multiple Governments, including ours, for banning trade with illegal settler occupied land." Its conclusion addressed the British government directly: "Punishing charities that deliver humanitarian aid and highlight gross human rights and international law should be a signal for our government to take stronger measures. Cowering to Israeli pressure will only cost more civilian lives. These are not the actions of an ally or a state that abides by international law. [Three observations, each narrower than the rhetoric around this episode. 15.3D A Member State Acting Alone, and Its Foreign Minister on What the Union Has Not Done: Ireland's Settlement-Goods Act and the "Erase a Population" Characterisation (15-22 September 2026)

The subsections above record a coordinated measure taken by twelve states and the assessment that it fell short of what the International Court of Justice's Advisory Opinion of 19 July 2024 requires. This subsection records the parallel case of a single member state legislating on its own account, the limits of what that legislation reaches, and the characterisation its foreign minister used one week later in New York - a characterisation that is materially stronger than the measure her own government has enacted, which is the point on which the entry turns.

The Act, and what it does. The Israeli Settlements in the Occupied Palestinian Territory (Prohibition of Importation of Goods) Act 2026 (Act No. 35 of 2026; Bill 57 of 2026) commenced on 15 September 2026. The text was approved by the Irish Government on 26 May 2026, passed all stages in the Oireachtas on 15 July 2026, and was signed into law by President Catherine Connolly on 23 July 2026; the commencement orders were signed by the Minister for Foreign Affairs and Trade, Helen McEntee TD. Section 3 empowers the Minister to designate by order the postal codes corresponding to Israeli settlements in territory brought under Israeli administration since June 1967, including East Jerusalem - the same codes the European Union already uses to verify eligibility for preferential tariff treatment under the EU-Israel Association Agreement, so that the machinery of enforcement is borrowed from an existing customs distinction rather than built new. Section 5 prohibits importation of goods originating in those codes; importation is an offence under section 14 of the Customs Act 2015, carrying that Act's powers of search, seizure and forfeiture. Section 6 provides an application route to Revenue for goods from postal codes lying partly inside and partly outside the occupied territory, and goods of a non-commercial nature in personal baggage are excluded. The measure descends from the Occupied Territories Bill introduced by Senator Frances Black in 2018, which no government advanced for seven years.

What the Act does not do, stated plainly. It covers goods only. An amendment to restore the prohibition on trade in services was defeated at Committee Stage in June 2026; services account for the substantial majority of Irish trade with Israel, and Al-Haq, welcoming the Act, recorded that the omission undermines its practical effect. The volume of settlement goods entering Ireland in the period 2020-2024 was under one million euro, so the measure's significance is not commercial. It is that a European Union member state has, for the first time, converted the ICJ's duty of non-assistance into a domestic criminal prohibition with a working customs mechanism - a precedent that can be copied, and whose absence elsewhere can now be measured against it.

The Minister on commencement (15 September 2026). Netanyahu's "kangaroo court" statement (July 2026) — and why it is legally baseless. On 19 July 2026, Netanyahu's Prime Minister's Office issued a formal statement characterising the ICC as "a kangaroo court that has no jurisdiction over Americans or Israelis," calling the arrest warrant "bogus" and alleging it was issued by former Prosecutor Karim Khan "a few days before allegations of sexual misconduct" as "a clear attempt by Khan to divert public attention." The statement was posted to the official @IsraeliPM account on X. The claim is false on every relevant point of law. First, the ICC was created by the Rome Statute, a treaty that now binds 125 states, and its judges are elected by those states — it is not a rogue body. Second, under Article 58, the prosecutor may only seek a warrant; three independent judges of the Pre-Trial Chamber must find reasonable grounds before one can issue — and they did, unanimously, on 21 November 2024. Khan personally neither convicted nor issued the warrants. Third, the claim that the ICC has "no jurisdiction over Israelis" is false: Article 12(2)(a) of the Rome Statute establishes territorial jurisdiction — crimes committed on the territory of a State Party fall within the Court's jurisdiction regardless of the accused's nationality. Palestine acceded to the Rome Statute in 2015, and Pre-Trial Chamber I ruled on 5 February 2021 that the Court's territorial jurisdiction extends to the territories occupied by Israel since 1967. A foreign national who commits a crime on a State Party's soil is not immunised by his passport. Fourth, under Article 86, all States Parties are bound to "cooperate fully with the Court" and, under Article 89(1), to comply with requests for arrest and surrender. The arrest warrant is not optional because the suspect is politically powerful. (ICC Pre-Trial Chamber I, 21 November 2024; Rome Statute Articles 12, 58, 86, 89; @IsraeliPM, 19 July 2026; Al Jazeera; NBC New York; CNBC.)

[The legal significance is in the structure of the position rather than in the sincerity of it. Palestinian self-determination is not an aspiration in international law but a right, and one the ICJ Advisory Opinion of 19 July 2024 treated as an obligation erga omnes, engaging every state; it is established in common Article 1 of the ICCPR and the ICESCR and in UN General Assembly Resolution 2625 (XXV). Point 19 recognises it as "the aspiration of the Palestinian people" rather than as their right, and conditions any pathway to it on the performance of others; the Secretary of State's gloss makes the condition explicit by locating statehood in "a negotiated deal with the Israelis." 15.9 The Collapse of Public Trust in the Media That Reported the War

The international verdict has been accompanied by a measurable collapse of public trust in the Western news institutions that covered the war. While the report identifies several drivers, the decline coincides with, and is widely attributed by audiences to, Western coverage of the Gaza genocide. (Reuters Institute Digital News Report 2026; Novara Media, April 2026; Centre for Media Monitoring; BBC staff open letters, 2024–2025.)

The Centre for Media Monitoring's underlying dataset supplies the granular figures behind that pattern: across a full year of coverage, the BBC used the word "murder" 220 times in reporting Israeli deaths against once for Palestinian deaths, and applied "massacre" to Israeli casualties 18 times more often than to Palestinian ones. (Centre for Media Monitoring; Media Bias Meter, "Framing Gaza," November 2025.)

A documented case of outright fabrication, not merely framing. 15.9A The New York Times as a Test Case: A Published Study, Its Own Disclaimers, and the Countervailing Record

This subsection is included because the coverage of a single newspaper has become the object of a published quantitative study, and because the characterisation placed on that study in political circulation differs from what its author wrote. Both are recorded, together with the separate body of findings on the same newspaper that runs in the opposite direction, so that the reader can compare them rather than receive either at second hand.

The study. Edieal J. Pinker, BearingPoint Professor of Operations Research at the Yale School of Management, published "How the New York Times Framed the War Between Israel and Hamas" in Studies in Conflict & Terrorism. The paper was received 14 May 2026, accepted 19 July 2026 and published online on 7 September 2026 as a Note rather than a full-length article, expanding a working paper posted to SSRN on 23 January 2025. Its corpus is 1,559 Times news articles mentioning both Israel and Gaza across the 244 days from 7 October 2023 to 7 June 2024, coded against a tagging protocol with inter-rater reliability checks and drawing on Entman's framing theory. Its central finding, in its own words, is "a dominant narrative consistent with a framing that assigns near-exclusive agency and responsibility to Israel while diminishing Hamas's role in sustaining the conflict," which it describes as omitting Israeli casualties after 7 October, Hamas fighter casualties, and Palestinian violence after 7 October. Supporting counts include "Israel" appearing 27,205 times against 8,499 for "Hamas"; Hamas fighters killed mentioned in only 12 per cent of ground articles; Israeli soldier deaths occurring on 128 days and mentioned on 61 of them; and personal accounts of Palestinian suffering appearing on 163 of the 244 days. (Edieal J. Pinker, Studies in Conflict & Terrorism, 7 September 2026, DOI 10.1080/1057610X.2026.2707956; SSRN 5104625.)

What the author expressly declines to claim. The paper states at page 6: "I do not make a claim that the NYTimes is 'biased' in its reporting." At page 7 it states that "this study does not attempt to directly address the question of bias." On the absence of a benchmark it asks, at page 16, "Is there a correct number of mentions? I cannot say." It closes: "I leave drawing conclusions about those questions to the reader." Its funding line records that the work "was supported by Yale School of Management," while The Times of Israel reported that "the study was not sponsored by Yale"; its disclosure line records that "no potential conflict of interest was reported by the author(s)," and the author has separately published an account of making aliyah and enlisting in the IDF in 1989, serving in Gaza the following year and later in the southern Lebanon security zone. He told The Times of Israel that he undertook the work because American readers "were not getting a clear understanding of how Israelis were experiencing the war." [The disclaimers are recorded because the paper's own scope is narrower than the use made of it, and the biography because the disclosure line reports none. Neither bears on whether the counts are accurate.]

The characterisation placed on it. The study was amplified almost entirely within Israeli, Jewish-communal and conservative American outlets. Israel National News reported it as proof of "deep and systematic" anti-Israel bias; The Times of Israel described it as a peer-reviewed study of the paper's "bias against Israel"; the American Israel Public Affairs Committee, in a post of 14-15 September 2026, stated that the analysis "concluded the outlet's reporting displayed clear bias against Israel," above a graphic reading "NEW YORK TIMES' GAZA WAR COVERAGE WAS CLEARLY BIASED AGAINST ISRAEL." [On the specific word, that is the opposite of the paper's text at pages 6 and 7. The distinction is not pedantic: the author declines the word because the claim would require a benchmark of unbiased coverage, and he states in terms that he has none.] (AIPAC, Facebook, 14-15 September 2026; Israel National News; The Times of Israel; JNS; The Jewish Chronicle; Israel Hayom, September 2026.)

What the same tables show when normalised by the dead. The study reports its ground-article counts (532 articles) against the casualty figures for each category. Divided through, per thousand dead: Israelis killed on 7 October, 1,195 dead, 303 articles, 253.6 per thousand; Israeli soldiers killed after 7 October, 364 dead, 59 articles, 162.1 per thousand; hostages taken, 251 people, 303 articles, 1,207.2 per thousand; Palestinians killed, 36,731 dead, 378 articles, 10.3 per thousand. The ratio between the first and last is 24.6 to 1, and between the second and last 15.7 to 1. On personal accounts the study records 111 Israeli stories against 1,590 Israeli dead (69.8 per thousand) and 207 Palestinian and Lebanese stories against 36,731 dead (5.6 per thousand), a ratio of 12.4 to 1. [The figures are the study's own and the operation applied to them is division. They do not refute its finding about narrative structure, which concerns what appears in the text rather than coverage per death, and the study does not claim to measure coverage per death. They bear on the characterisation placed on it by third parties, because a dataset in which a dead Israeli attracts roughly twenty-five times the coverage of a dead Palestinian is a difficult foundation for the claim that the coverage was "clearly biased against Israel."]

The countervailing record on the same newspaper. Three separate bodies of work, using different methods and published before this study, reach findings on The New York Times that run the other way, and they are set out at length in the companion analysis `western-media-bias-analysis.md`. The Intercept (Adam Johnson and Othman Ali, January 2024) examined more than a thousand articles in the Times, The Washington Post and the Los Angeles Times across the first six weeks of the war and found that the word "massacre" was applied almost exclusively to Israeli deaths, that Palestinian deaths were reported predominantly in passive constructions and aggregate figures, and that mentions of antisemitism outnumbered mentions of Islamophobia by 549 to 79. And in April 2024 The Intercept published a leaked internal Times style memo, first circulated in November 2023 over the names of standards editor Susan Wessling and international editor Philip Pan, instructing reporters covering Gaza to avoid the words "genocide" and "ethnic cleansing," to avoid "occupied territory," to use "Palestine" only in very rare cases, and to avoid "refugee camps." On 21 September 2026 the British journalist Owen Jones published "Ed Sheeran just made everything worse", subtitled "His 'apology' was an absolute calamity, frankly", having written earlier on X of "the absolute cowardice of @edsheeran." His criticisms are commentary and are recorded as such: that the apology named neither the decision being apologised for nor the party injured by it; that it treated 7 October 2023 as the beginning of the account while placing Jewish suffering in the context of "centuries of pain", so that the Palestinian history recorded in Parts II, VIII and IX of this report does not appear at all; that the tearful passage about personal abandonment inverted the position Macklemore had taken in writing on 14 September ("I am not a victim. Ed Sheeran is not a victim"), which Jones characterised as "cowardice piled on self-pity piled on amorality"; and that the closing apology for having raised the subject undid whatever the opening apology had done.

One element of that critique is not commentary but a comparison of two dated statements, and it is recorded here on that basis. On 15 September 2026 the written statement said of Macklemore's removal: "was the promoter's decision, it was not mine" (§15.12). On 19 September 2026, from the stage, the speaker said: "I've had to take and make difficult decisions, and I am making mistakes, and I am so, so sorry." The first locates the decision with another party; the second describes it as his own and apologises for it. No reconciliation of the two has been offered, and the question of which party could have refused - left open at §15.12, where the promoter, the venues and the artist each place the decision elsewhere - is not closed by either. Separately, the temporal asymmetry Jones identifies reaches by a different route the same place as the grammatical asymmetry recorded above: whether the measure is which events are allowed to begin the account, or which sentences carry a named actor, the Israeli side of the ledger is given both a history and an agent and the Palestinian side is given neither. (Owen Jones, "Ed Sheeran just made everything worse", owenjones.news, 21 September 2026; Owen Jones on X; Philadelphia Inquirer, 20 September 2026; Yahoo Entertainment, 20 September 2026.)

The opening address named the conduct, named the West Bank, and said the speaker could no longer hide how he felt; the closing address, delivered unscripted at the end of the same show, contained no reference to Gaza, to the West Bank, to Palestinians or to the war, and ended by apologising for the opening address - the apology being for having spoken about it, and directed to the audience rather than to the removed performer. The sequence is therefore that the last thing said on the subject that evening was a regret at having raised it, offered some two hours after the statement that it could no longer be left unsaid. What is also recorded is the grammar, because it is checkable and because it recurs: across two statements eleven days apart, prepared separately, Israel is named where the subject is the attack of 7 October or the conflict in the abstract, and unnamed where the subject is the conduct in Gaza. 15.13 The Architecture of Non-Accountability: Press Exclusion and Sanctions on the Court

Two measures, taken by different states and directed at different institutions, share a single effect: they act not on the conduct this report documents but on the capacity of independent bodies to establish it. They are recorded together because they bear on the evidentiary standing of every figure in this document.

The exclusion of independent international press from Gaza. Since 7 October 2023 Israel has barred independent access to the Gaza Strip by international journalists. Foreign correspondents have entered only as embeds under Israeli military escort, with material subject to military review; no independent international reporting from inside Gaza has been permitted at any point. In January 2024 Israel's Supreme Court rejected an appeal by international media organisations for independent access, accepting the state's argument that the presence of journalists could expose operational details such as troop positions. The Foreign Press Association (FPA), which represents more than 350 correspondents working in Israel and the Palestinian territories, filed a fresh petition in September 2024, arguing that a blanket prohibition violates press freedom, the public's right to information and journalists' right to practise their profession, and that it "contravenes the foundational principles of the state as a democratic country." The state obtained seven deferrals in the petition's first year, each granted. At a hearing on 23 October 2025 the state conceded that the ceasefire was a material change of circumstances; the court directed it to state a clear position and granted a further thirty days, after which the state reiterated that entry without military accompaniment remained too dangerous. A hearing was held in January 2026, and the case stalled again after the state was permitted to file classified material not disclosed to the petitioners; the deputy president said a decision would follow at a later date. On 13 April 2026 lawyers for the FPA, Reporters Without Borders (RSF), the Committee to Protect Journalists (CPJ) and the Union of Journalists in Israel filed an emergency motion to shorten the state's deadline, which the court had already moved from 31 March to 24 May 2026, arguing that the stated grounds no longer obtained: no hostages remained in Gaza, a ceasefire was in place, and no substantiated threat to journalists had been identified. FPA chair Tania Kraemer stated: "This indefinite ban must end," the successive delays having "made a mockery of the legal process." CPJ chief executive Jodie Ginsberg noted that Israel had by then prohibited independent international media access for more than 900 days. (RSF, petition filings and statements, 2024–2026; CPJ, 13 April 2026 and subsequent; Times of Israel, 23 October 2025; +972 Magazine, "The legal fight to open Gaza to foreign press has failed.")

[The evidentiary significance is structural rather than rhetorical. The casualty totals, famine determinations and destruction assessments recorded throughout this report are contested in Western political debate principally on the ground that they originate with the Gaza Ministry of Health, with UN agencies or with Palestinian journalists rather than with independent international correspondents present on the ground. That absence is not a feature of the conflict; it is a policy, maintained through four years of litigation by the state whose conduct is in question, and maintained alongside the killing of the local journalists who remained - 294+ Palestinian journalists recorded by the UN Human Rights Office as of April 2026, 263+ journalists and media workers across all theatres on CPJ's stricter methodology, at least 64 directly targeted, making Israel the deadliest force for journalists CPJ has recorded since it began documentation in 1992 (§6.3). The same exclusion that prevents independent verification is then invoked to dispute the figures that exist. South Africa placed precisely this in issue before the ICJ in its Article 11 non-compliance dossier of 25 August 2026 (§6.6), accusing Israel of actively suppressing evidence of its own conduct by killing local journalists, barring foreign media and preventing UN-mandated investigative bodies from entering Gaza.]

United States sanctions on the International Criminal Court and on UN mandate-holders. On 6 February 2025 President Donald Trump signed Executive Order 14203, "Imposing Sanctions on the International Criminal Court," citing the Court's "illegitimate and baseless actions targeting America and our close ally Israel." The order authorises asset freezes and entry bans, and, through the International Emergency Economic Powers Act of 1977, exposes US persons who provide substantial support to the designated proceedings to fines of up to $1,000,000 and up to twenty years' imprisonment. It followed the Pre-Trial Chamber's finding of 21 November 2024 that there were reasonable grounds to believe Netanyahu and Gallant bore criminal responsibility for war crimes and crimes against humanity (§15.5).

The designations were then made in sequence: then-Prosecutor Karim Khan KC under the order itself; four judges on 5 June 2025; UN Special Rapporteur Francesca Albanese on 9 July 2025, the first time a serving UN special procedures mandate-holder has been sanctioned by a member state, which the UN and independent experts have characterised as a violation of the 1946 Convention on the Privileges and Immunities of the United Nations; both Deputy Prosecutors and two further judges on 20 August 2025, namely Deputy Prosecutors Nazhat Shameem Khan (Fiji) and Mame Mandiaye Niang (Senegal) and Judges Kimberly Prost (Canada) and Nicolas Guillou (France); the Palestinian human rights organisations Al-Haq, the Al Mezan Center for Human Rights and the Palestinian Centre for Human Rights on 4 September 2025, added to the Treasury's Specially Designated Nationals list by OFAC for having filed evidence and observations with the Court; two further judges on 18 December 2025; and, on 18 August 2026, ICC President Tomoko Akane (Japan) and senior trial lawyer Abdoulaye Seye (Senegal), the latter reported to have served on the prosecution team that sought the Netanyahu warrant. The cumulative effect is that nine of the Court's eighteen judges, both Deputy Prosecutors, its former Prosecutor and its serving President have been sanctioned by the United States. Secretary of State Marco Rubio stated that those designated "have directly engaged in efforts by the ICC to investigate, arrest, detain, or prosecute officials whose government has not consented to ICC jurisdiction." The Court described the measures as "a flagrant attack against the independence of an impartial judicial institution." Judge Prost has reported being unable to access her Google and Amazon accounts or to use her credit card. On 13 May 2026 the United States District Court for the District of Columbia suspended the sanctions against Albanese pending a ruling on the merits; they remain in force against the judges, the former Prosecutor and his aides, and the three Palestinian organisations. (Executive Order 14203, 6 February 2025; US Department of State, "Sanctioning Lawfare that Targets U.S. and Israeli Persons," 9 July 2025; US Department of the Treasury (OFAC), SDN designations, 4 September 2025; Human Rights Watch, 4 September 2025 and 19 August 2026; OHCHR, 22 September 2025; International Criminal Court, "The ICC strongly rejects new US sanctions designations," 18 August 2026; Al Jazeera and France 24, 18 August 2026; Just Security; Legal Agenda, on the DC district court suspension of 13 May 2026.)

[The legal significance is that the object of the measure is the adjudicating body rather than the allegation. No designation contests a finding of fact or of law made by the Court, and none is framed as doing so; the stated ground in each case is participation in the proceedings themselves. Read together with the press exclusion above, the pattern is consistent: where the record is not disputed on its merits, the institutions capable of establishing it - the resident press, the international press, the Court, the special procedures of the Human Rights Council, and the Palestinian documentation organisations that supply them - are acted upon instead. This bears directly on the "wait for a final determination" position addressed at §6.6 and §15.8: the same measures that counsel waiting are applied to the bodies whose determination is awaited.]

15.13A The Exemption Stated in the First Person: A Governing-Party Legislator on the Application of International Law (16 September 2026)

The measures recorded in §15.13 operate on the institutions that would establish the record. This section records the same position stated openly, as a proposition, by a serving legislator of the governing party.

The remarks. On Wednesday 16 September 2026, speaking about the mission and religious identity of the Jewish people, Hanoch Milwidsky, a Member of the Knesset for Likud, the party of the Prime Minister, said: "The land of Israel was given to us because we are supposed to fulfil a mission here." The mission, he said, is "to establish a Jewish state here that will be worthy of Jews." From that he drew the operative conclusion: "We are not like everyone else, and therefore all the laws that apply to everyone cannot apply to us." He added that "the considerations of all kinds of statesmen from around the world should not be relevant to us" and that "we must remain connected here to our sources," invoking the biblical formulation of "a people that shall dwell alone" (Numbers 23:9) and what he termed "separateness." He described the people of Israel as "a distinguished people, because they have an obligation to create an exemplary model here in the land of Israel." The remarks were reported by Israel's Channel 13 and circulated on video from 17 September 2026. (Channel 13, 16-17 September 2026; Anadolu Agency, 18 September 2026; Middle East Monitor, 17 September 2026; Middle East Eye, 19 September 2026.)

[The wording is a translation from Hebrew and no full independently verified English transcript has been published; the renderings differ in phrasing between outlets while agreeing on the operative sentence, and it is recorded here on that basis. The claim is about the applicability of law, not about any particular operation, and it is recorded in this Part rather than in the dolus specialis record at §6.2 for that reason.]

The same speaker on the same principle applied to a detainee (29 July 2024). The position has a documented prior application. On 29 July 2024, after military police detained nine soldiers on suspicion of raping a Palestinian detainee at Sde Teiman - an assault that left the detainee with a ruptured bowel, broken ribs and lung damage (§10.3) - Milwidsky walked out of a Knesset Finance Committee vote in protest at the arrests, calling on coalition members to follow him and depriving the committee of its quorum. Asked by Hadash-Ta'al MK Ahmad Tibi whether it was legitimate to insert an object into a detainee's rectum, he replied: "Yes! If he is a Nukhba, everything is legitimate to do! Everything!" (CBS News, 30 July 2024; Jerusalem Post, 29-30 July 2024, rendering the reply more briefly as "If he is a Nukhba, everything is legitimate"; Al Jazeera, 9 August 2024.)

[The legal significance is that the proposition is expressly foreclosed by the law it addresses, and by treaties Israel has ratified. Article 27 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (1969) provides that a party "may not invoke the provisions of its internal law as justification for its failure to perform a treaty" - and internal law is a stronger ground than the one advanced here, which is not law at all. The prohibitions at issue are jus cogens: the prohibition of genocide, of apartheid, of torture and of crimes against humanity are peremptory norms from which no derogation is permitted, and they bind states irrespective of consent. Common Article 1 of the four Geneva Conventions obliges every High Contracting Party to respect and ensure respect for the Conventions "in all circumstances"; Article 2(2) of the Convention against Torture, which Israel ratified in 1991, provides that "no exceptional circumstances whatsoever" may be invoked to justify torture. The 2024 remark is the doctrine applied to a person: the detainee's designation was asserted, and from the assertion an exemption from the absolute prohibition was derived - the same structure as the designation-by-assertion documented at §6.7 and §9.4. Two further points are recorded because the record is the place for them rather than the argument. First, the speaker is not a fringe figure but a member of the governing party's Knesset delegation, which places the statement within the category of official rather than public discourse. Second, the proposition that Jews as such stand outside the laws that bind other peoples is, when asserted by anyone other than its author, the paradigm case of what the state and its advocates identify as antisemitism (Part XXI); here it is advanced by an elected representative of the state as the reason the state need not answer for its conduct.]
